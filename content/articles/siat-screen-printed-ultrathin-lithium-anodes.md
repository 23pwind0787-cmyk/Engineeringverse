Lithium-metal anodes are the obvious route to higher-energy batteries: lithium packs more charge per gram than the graphite used today. The catch is making them thin. Conventional lithium foils are 50 micrometres or thicker and carry 15–25% excess inactive lithium, and roll pressing struggles to make foils below 20 micrometres without cracking. A team at the Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology (SIAT), Chinese Academy of Sciences, has found a way around that — by printing the lithium instead of rolling it.

## Printing through a mask

The method, reported by Liu, Wei, Ning and colleagues in *Nano-Micro Letters* (19, 37, 2027; published online 21 August 2026, open access), forces molten lithium mixed with Mg(TFSI)₂ through a stainless-steel mask onto copper foil. It forms evenly spaced discrete lithium microdisc arrays instead of a continuous film — a design the authors call Li@Mg(TFSI)₂. That matters because molten lithium wets copper poorly (contact angle above 90°), which makes uniform thin coatings otherwise impossible.

The equivalent thicknesses — 10, 20 and 28 micrometres — are controlled by the microdisc size, spacing and thickness. The method was demonstrated on copper sheets of about 30 × 10 cm, so this is a lab-scale demonstration, not production.

## Why it works

The Mg(TFSI)₂ reacts with the molten lithium in situ to form LiF, Li₃N, Li₂S and Li–Mg compounds, creating an inorganic-rich solid electrolyte interphase that homogenises lithium deposition, suppresses dendrites and cuts active-lithium loss. The anodes also showed promising results in gel-electrolyte solid-state cells, not just liquid-electrolyte ones.

The cycling numbers come from liquid-electrolyte full cells with high-loading LFP cathodes (12.3 mg/cm², about 2.0 mAh/cm², and 20.0 mg/cm², about 3.3 mAh/cm²). The 10-micrometre anode maintained specific capacity above 130 mAh/g and Coulombic efficiency above 99.8% over 260 cycles.

## The 504 Wh/kg figure — calculated, not measured

Paired with high-loading nickel-rich cathodes (NCM811 and Ni92 were the ones tested), the paper's calculated stack-level energy densities reach 504 Wh/kg and 1,071 Wh/L for the 20-micrometre-equivalent configuration. That is a calculation from the cell stack, not a measured energy density from a built cell — the word "calculated" has to stay attached to the figure. Even so, it shows what ultra-thin anodes could unlock: less dead lithium, more of the cell's weight doing useful work, and a printing route that sidesteps the cracking limit of foil rolling.
