Russia's missile arsenal is not one weapon but five different answers to five different problems: a low-flying cruise missile, a battlefield ballistic missile, an air-launched ballistic missile, a scramjet cruise missile and an intermediate-range missile with multiple warheads. Here is how they compare — and, for the hypersonic claims, what the evidence actually supports.

One rule for the whole comparison: every speed and range below is a Russian claim unless it is explicitly labelled as an independent estimate or a combat observation. Those are different things, and for these weapons the difference matters.

## The five at a glance

| System | Type | Launched from | Claimed speed | Claimed range | Warhead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kalibr (3M14) | Land-attack cruise missile | Ships, submarines | Subsonic (~Mach 0.8) | 1,500–2,500 km | ~450 kg |
| Iskander-M (9M723) | Short-range ballistic missile | Road-mobile launcher | Mach 6–7 | ~500 km | ~480 kg |
| Kinzhal (Kh-47M2) | Air-launched ballistic missile | MiG-31K | Mach 10 | 2,000 km* | ~500 kg (est.) |
| Zircon (3M22) | Scramjet cruise missile | Frigates, submarines, coastal launchers | Mach 8–9 | 1,000 km | 200–300 kg (claimed) |
| Oreshnik | Intermediate-range ballistic missile | Road-mobile launcher | Above Mach 10 | ~5,000 km | Multiple warheads |

\* The Kinzhal's 2,000 km figure includes the carrying aircraft's flight. The missile's own range once released is estimated at 460–480 km.

## Kalibr: the workhorse

The Kalibr is the least exotic of the five and the most used. The land-attack version, the 3M14, is a subsonic cruise missile launched from ships and submarines, flying low like a small unmanned aircraft to strike targets 1,500–2,500 km away with a warhead of about 450 kg. A separate anti-ship version, the 3M54, goes supersonic in its terminal phase.

It has been fired extensively in Ukraine, mainly against infrastructure, and its performance is the least disputed of the five: subsonic cruise missiles are well-understood technology, and the Kalibr is roughly a Russian equivalent of the US Tomahawk. Its advantage is range and numbers, not speed — and at subsonic speed it is also the most interceptable.

## Iskander-M: the battlefield missile

The Iskander-M is a short-range ballistic missile fired from a road-mobile launcher, with a range of about 500 km and a warhead of about 480 kg, although some sources cite up to 800 kg for newer variants. It flies a quasi-ballistic trajectory and can manoeuvre in its terminal phase, which is designed to defeat missile defences such as Patriot and THAAD.

It is the most combat-proven system in the group, used throughout the war in Ukraine, and its figures are the least contested: 500 km is also the range Russia declared for it, and the closely related Iskander-K cruise missile variant shares the same launcher and a similar range. Its limitation is reach — 500 km keeps it a theatre weapon, not a strategic one.

## Kinzhal: the air-launched Iskander

The Kinzhal, meaning "dagger", is an adapted Iskander-M fitted for air launch. A MiG-31K interceptor carries it to high altitude and supersonic speed before release, which adds range and energy. Russia claims a top speed of Mach 10 and a reach of 2,000 km — but that range includes the aircraft's flight; the missile itself is estimated to fly 460–480 km after release. It entered service in 2017 and was first used in Ukraine in March 2022.

Its reputation for invulnerability ended in May 2023, when a Ukrainian Patriot battery shot one down over Kyiv — an interception later confirmed by the Pentagon. More have been intercepted since, though success depends on the battery being close to the target and having enough interceptors. Ukrainian military intelligence estimated in mid-2026 that Russia held about 50 Kinzhals and was building about five a month.

Chinese analysts who studied its performance in Ukraine in 2023 concluded it is not really a hypersonic missile: it follows a ballistic trajectory and cannot manoeuvre at hypersonic speed. A US Defense Intelligence Agency report noted that Russia upgraded the Iskander and Kinzhal in spring 2025 with a terminal-phase manoeuvring capability, in order to bypass Patriot systems. Either way, the Kinzhal is best understood as a very fast ballistic missile — not a weapon that sustains hypersonic flight while manoeuvring.

## Zircon: the scramjet gamble

The Zircon is the most technically ambitious of the five. A rocket booster accelerates it until an air-breathing scramjet engine can take over, and it then cruises at a claimed Mach 8 to 9 at high altitude. It was designed to sink ships and is carried by Admiral Gorshkov-class frigates and Yasen-M submarines in the same launch cells as the Kalibr. Russia says its range is about 1,000 km.

Since early 2024 Russia has fired Zircons at land targets in Ukraine, apparently from coastal launchers in Crimea. Ukraine says it has intercepted some. Independent estimates are lower than the claims: Ukrainian analysts at Defence Express put its maximum range at about 700 km, with a steady-state speed around Mach 5.5, accelerating to about Mach 7.5 near the target and slowing to about Mach 4.5 close to the ground. They also estimate its warhead at 100–150 kg, against the claimed 200–300 kg — and note poor manoeuvrability, which makes its trajectory easier to predict. Even on the lower estimates, though, it is still a hypersonic figure, and it is difficult — not impossible — to shoot down. Patriot and SM-6 interceptors are among the systems assessed as capable of engaging it.

Stocks were put at about 120 in mid-2026, with output of about three a month.

## Oreshnik: the newest and most publicised

The Oreshnik is an intermediate-range ballistic missile derived from the RS-26 Rubezh. It carries six warheads, each releasing several submunitions, which arrive at more than Mach 10. Russia claims a range of about 5,000 km. It was first fired at an industrial site in Dnipro in November 2024, reportedly struck an aircraft repair plant in the Lviv region in January 2026, and was launched twice more in May 2026. Russia placed it on combat duty in Belarus in December 2025.

Russia calls it hypersonic, which is accurate for the speed of its warheads — and true of every missile of that range. Western and Ukrainian analysts see it mainly as a signal: a weapon that can reach any European capital in minutes. The harder problem is defensive: its warheads separate outside the atmosphere, and Ukraine has no system built to engage targets there. Interceptors of that kind exist — the American SM-3 and THAAD and the Israeli Arrow 3 — but they are few and expensive.

## So which hypersonic claims hold up?

Hypersonic means faster than Mach 5, five times the speed of sound. Speed alone is not new — long-range ballistic missiles have exceeded it since the 1950s. What defines the modern category is sustained hypersonic flight inside the atmosphere combined with manoeuvre, which makes the weapon hard to track and intercept. By that stricter definition, only the Zircon — and the nuclear Avangard glide vehicle, which is not one of these five — are hypersonic weapons in the new sense. The Kinzhal and the Oreshnik are ballistic missiles with some ability to manoeuvre.

The honest summary: the weapons work, but in Ukraine they have not been decisive. They are produced in small numbers — a handful a month — and the fastest of them have been shot down. Their main value is strategic: they complicate NATO missile defence and give Moscow a tool for intimidation short of nuclear use.
