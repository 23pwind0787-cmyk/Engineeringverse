The US Navy is preparing a new production capability at its China Lake weapons facility in California to help supply warheads for Raytheon's expanding Tomahawk production. The effort includes a new industrial mixer, rebuilt manufacturing facilities and the qualification of an additional warhead producer.

The Naval Air Warfare Center Weapons Division (NAWCWD) recently completed seven first-article Tomahawk warheads for inspection — the first units a new producer builds to be checked. Once they pass, they become China Lake's first production warheads. On 9 November, the Navy cut the ribbon on a new 300-gallon mixer in the rebuilt Motor Assembly Compound. Once online around June 2027, the mixer will let the team reach its planned production rate, with warheads ready before Raytheon needs them.

## Why the Navy is making warheads itself

The expansion follows the Navy's August 2026 award of a roughly $22.9 billion, seven-year contract to Raytheon to accelerate Tomahawk production — covering more than 7,000 new-build, recertified and modernised missiles, with annual production targeted to exceed 1,000 rounds.

Faster production needs more warhead capacity. Raytheon is the prime contractor for Tomahawk, General Dynamics Ordnance and Tactical Systems machines the warhead cases, and L3Harris currently loads them with explosive. The Navy wants China Lake to take on a portion of that work through a public-private partnership with L3Harris, signed in September 2026, so the service has another way to respond to rising demand. China Lake's role is intended to supplement existing industrial production, not replace it.

Preparing the additional source took more than installing machinery. The team spent much of the past year updating the warhead specification and its approved supplier list — the Navy's technical authority must approve each new supplier — and bought long-lead materials in advance, since some components can take up to a year to arrive. More than a dozen new employees are joining the energetics team and will train for about a year under experienced technicians before taking on independent production.

## A mixer twice the size, in a rebuilt compound

The new mixer, built by B&P Littleford, is designed for the thick propellants and explosives used in military energetics manufacturing. Its 300-gallon capacity is twice the 150-gallon mixer the team has run for decades, and its bowl will weigh close to 20,000 pounds.

The new facility layout also cuts handling time. Previously, crews mixed materials in one building and transported them about half a mile to another for casting — an hour-long move. In the rebuilt compound, the mixing bowl moves across the same facility into a lift, cutting the transfer to around 15 minutes. That matters because the material has a limited processing window after mixing — a couple of hours to eight or nine hours, depending on the mix — so the new arrangement recovers about 45 minutes for subsequent production steps.

The facilities are part of a wider reconstruction: earthquakes in July 2019 destroyed three energetics compounds in the Salt Wells area of China Lake, and construction of their replacements began in April 2022. The 9 November ceremony marked a major milestone in the rebuilt complex.

The energetics team has decades of practice — it has filled warheads for the air-launched SLAM-ER missile since the mid-1990s. NAWCWD describes the facility as a pilot plant that can support developmental work while augmenting industry when additional capacity is needed. The immediate objective is to qualify China Lake as a reliable source of Tomahawk warheads, so a shortage of components does not slow the wider missile production effort.
