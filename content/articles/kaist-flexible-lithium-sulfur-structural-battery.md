A structural battery does two jobs at once: it carries mechanical load and stores energy, so the structure of a drone or robot stops being dead weight. A team at KAIST, led by Professor Il-Kwon Oh in the Department of Mechanical Engineering with first author Jawon Ha, has built a flexible lithium-sulfur electrode designed for exactly that — and then bent it, loaded it, and broken its frame to see what happens.

## Nanowires grown on cloth

The electrode is made by growing NiCo₂Se₄ (nickel cobalt selenide) nanowires directly onto woven carbon cloth, through growth and selenization of a nickel–cobalt precursor. The result is a binder-free, monolithically integrated cathode scaffold the team calls NCSe@CC. As a lithium-sulfur cathode it delivers 1,544 mAh/g at 0.1 C with a polarisation of just 0.13 V; the nanowires also trap the lithium polysulfides that normally plague Li-S cells and improve sulfur's conductivity.

For flexibility, the electrode went through 1,000 curvature-conditioning cycles at a bend radius of 2.5 mm. The nanowire–fibre integration was retained, with no visible damage or collapse, and pouch cells built with conditioned electrodes discharged comparably to cells with pristine electrodes. Under cylindrical curvature, a pouch cell retains 94.0% of its flat-state capacity.

## The frame broke; the voltage barely moved

The striking test is the stepped loading test. The pouch cell was loaded in 5, 10 and 15 mm steps with 10-second holds. Voltage retention was 99.96%, 99.835% and 99.828% — the "99.8%" headline figure is the rounded worst step. The cell voltage moved only from 2.457 V to 2.452 V, about 5 mV, even as the surrounding PLA demonstrator frame fractured during loading, and an LED powered by the cell stayed lit without flickering. To be precise: this is voltage retention under load up to frame fracture, not capacity retention.

## Drone legs: demonstrated as a component, not a vehicle

The team integrated pouch cells into cylindrical, oval, square and drone-leg geometries and powered an LED after insertion and fixation — a real demonstrator at component level, not a flying drone or a working robot. Weight saved and flight-time gains are explicitly not yet quantified; that is future work, with load-bearing composites. Co-authors include Leif E. Asp of Chalmers, from a well-known structural-battery group.

The work is published as "Monolithically integrated NiCo₂Se₄ nanowire–carbon cloth cathode scaffolds for non-planar lithium-sulfur structural-battery integration" in *Advanced Composites and Hybrid Materials* 9(5), article 414 (2026), with a KAIST news release on 18 November 2026. The claim, stated carefully, is strong enough: an electrode you can bend a thousand times, build into a frame, and break the frame around — without the battery noticing.
