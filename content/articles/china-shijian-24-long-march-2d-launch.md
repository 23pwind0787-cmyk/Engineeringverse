China has added another experimental spacecraft to its technology-testing fleet — and, characteristically for the series, said almost nothing about what it is for. Shijian-24 (实践二十四号, "Practice-24") lifted off from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center in Shanxi, Launch Complex 9, at 06:51 Beijing time on Thursday 19 November 2026 (22:51 UTC, 18 November), aboard a Long March 2D (vehicle Y106, built by the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology). The satellite entered its planned orbit and the mission was declared a complete success.

It was the 678th flight of the Long March series and the 108th flight of the Long March 2D. The payload flew under a 3.8 m-diameter fairing, and the cold weather at Taiyuan required extra insulation and pre-launch heating measures.

## What was not said

Xinhua's report stated no mission objectives at all. That part of the story is exactly as it appears: the Shijian ("Practice") series is China's technology-testing and experimental line, so describing Shijian-24 as an experimental spacecraft joining that fleet is fair — but nothing more specific is on the record.

Two details in the wider coverage need qualification:

- **"Sun-synchronous orbit" is not officially stated.** Xinhua said only that the satellite entered its "preset/planned orbit" and named no orbit type. The sun-synchronous designation comes from specialist trackers and aggregator coverage; no published orbital elements (altitude or inclination) for Shijian-24 were found in the sources checked, so the label is credible but unconfirmed by tracking data. (Care is needed not to confuse Shijian-24 with Shiyan-24, a different satellite launched in 2023.)
- **"No manufacturer disclosed" is true of the announcement, with a caveat.** Neither Xinhua nor the launch reports named a builder. However, the specialist outlet China in Space reports that the China Academy of Space Technology (CAST) indicated via WeChat, hours after deployment, that it worked on the spacecraft. So the accurate phrasing is "not named in the official launch announcement", rather than wholly undisclosed.

That is the whole story, and it is a familiar one: a successful launch, a milestone count, and a satellite whose job will only become clear — if it ever does — from watching what it does in orbit.
