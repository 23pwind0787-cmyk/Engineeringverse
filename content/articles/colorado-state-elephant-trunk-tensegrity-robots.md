Researchers at Colorado State University have developed flexible robots inspired by elephant trunks that can bend, grip objects and connect with each other — with the goal of one day sending teams of them to help with space exploration, manufacturing or search and rescue.

The robots are described in detail in the journal *Nature Machine Intelligence*. They were developed by Associate Professor Jianguo Zhao and his team in CSU's Department of Mechanical Engineering, and they are designed to address a limitation of current robotic systems, which are usually built from rigid blocks that limit the shapes they can form.

## A body with no joints

The new machines use a structural design called tensegrity, in which stiff rods are held in place by a web of tensioned cables rather than being rigidly locked together. That makes them flexible: they can bend, collapse, stretch and change shape as needed. Like tentacles, they have no traditional joints — yet they feature a pair of gripping claws at both ends of their tube-like bodies.

The dual claws are central to the system. Individual robots can use them to grasp objects, or to attach to partner robots and form larger structures — without relying on magnets, which can sometimes fail as connection points. The mechanical claw attachment also reduces the chance of misalignment when units connect.

> "Often the physical structure of robots is fixed, but our approach allows these flexible systems to adapt their shape to their environment and to the potentially changing needs of their mission by collapsing, stretching or grasping. They are also modular, meaning each robot works like a building block: it can operate on its own or connect with others to form bigger, more capable shapes." — Jianguo Zhao, Associate Professor of Mechanical Engineering, Colorado State University

Once joined, the robots can form chains, loops and branching structures of different sizes. Each arrangement offers different possibilities for movement and manipulation, letting the collective adapt its physical structure to the task at hand.

## Movement and manipulation in one body

The robots combine movement and object handling — a capability the team calls loco-manipulation. Rather than needing separate mechanisms for navigating terrain and manipulating objects, they can use their flexible bodies and gripping claws for both. They can bend around objects to lift and carry loads of different sizes, and they demonstrated multimodal locomotion, using different movement strategies to navigate varied environments.

The team tested the robots in the laboratory and in the field, taking them across natural landscapes to see how they handled real terrain. One of the hardest engineering challenges was selecting motors small enough to fit inside the robots while still powerful and reliable enough for the various movements.

The robots are not yet fully autonomous. They currently need a human pilot for most operations, although the team has demonstrated that two robots can find each other and connect on their own.

> "Right now, these systems need to be piloted by a human. However, we have already demonstrated that two robots can find each other and connect on their own. In the future, we can envision mostly autonomous teams of tens or even hundreds of them — attached through the claws — traveling to a location and then deploying for a specific purpose." — Mahmud Hasan Saikot, doctoral student at CSU and co-first author

Future work will focus on improving autonomy, increasing operating speeds and enabling the robots to work alongside humans with less supervision. Such collectives could travel to a designated location before rearranging themselves into configurations suited to a specific operation — in manufacturing, space exploration or search-and-rescue missions, where robots may need to navigate unpredictable environments, manipulate objects and adapt to changing requirements.

The paper's co-first authors are Mahmud Hasan Saikot, a doctoral student at CSU who took over the robot initially designed by team member Sydney Spiegel. Other CSU-affiliated authors include Sudheera Akalanka Kariyawasam, Andrew Stefka and Josh Chrisler.
