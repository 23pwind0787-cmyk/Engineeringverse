Inside a neutron star, matter is squeezed into something almost entirely made of neutrons. Recreating even a fleeting version of that on Earth — a system of neutrons with no protons at all — is one of nuclear physics' standing quests, sometimes called the search for "atomic number zero". A Japan–Korea collaboration has now sharpened one of the key instruments for that hunt.

## The detector move

The work is led at the RIKEN Nishina Center for Accelerator-Based Science by Group Director Tomohiro Uesaka, in a new collaboration with South Korea's Institute for Basic Science (IBS), with Tohoku University (Associate Professor Kenjiro Miki leads the multineutron experiments) and TU Darmstadt also involved.

The instrument is LAMPS-NDA, the neutron detector array built for the LAMPS spectrometer at IBS's RAON accelerator in Korea (LAMPS project leads: Byungsik Hong and Young Jin Kim). Its detector depth is 80 cm, against 48 cm for RIKEN's existing NEBULA array. In 2025 LAMPS-NDA was relocated from South Korea to RIKEN and integrated with the detector system at the SAMURAI spectrometer at RIBF, RIKEN's Radioactive Isotope Beam Factory. Joint accelerator experiments officially began in May 2026: an ¹⁸O beam strikes a beryllium target, an ⁸He beam is separated by BigRIPS, and that beam hits a liquid hydrogen target in the search for tetraneutron (4n) and hexaneutron (6n) systems.

## 3.5% — reached in stages, and worth defining

The forwarded version says efficiency jumped "from about 0.1% to 3.5%". The real path, per RIKEN, has three steps: 0.1% was the tetraneutron detection efficiency with NEBULA alone; an intermediate upgrade with France's LPC Caen, doubling NEBULA's depth from 48 cm, raised it to slightly above 1%; adding LAMPS-NDA then nearly doubled it to 3.5%. For six-neutron events, efficiency rose 2.5 times to 0.44%.

The percentage also needs its definition: it is the efficiency for simultaneously detecting all four neutrons emitted in a candidate event — the direct-detection approach — not a generic efficiency figure. Small numbers, but in this field they are the difference between a hint and a measurement.

## Nothing created — yet

The goal, in Uesaka's words, is "to create, for the first time on Earth, matter composed almost entirely of neutrons, similar to the material found inside neutron stars" — to probe the three-neutron force and why neutron stars do not collapse. But only the detector integration and the start of joint experiments are demonstrated so far. No tetraneutron or hexaneutron detection with the new setup, and no neutron-only matter, has been reported. Earlier tetraneutron evidence came from previous RIKEN experiments — a 2016 candidate resonance and a 2022 correlated four-neutron system at SAMURAI published in *Nature* — using missing-mass reconstruction, a different method from the direct detection now being attempted.
