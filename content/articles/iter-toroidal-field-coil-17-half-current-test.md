ITER has taken a major step toward its first plasma. Toroidal field coil #17 — one of the giant D-shaped superconducting magnets that will form the tokamak's magnetic cage — has been successfully energised to 34,000 amps, half its nominal operating current of 68,000 amps, after being cooled to 4 K, or minus 269 °C. The coil was removed from ITER's Magnet Cold Test Facility on 30 October 2026, and the result was announced on 2 November.

This was a staged test, not the final one. Full current produces far larger electromagnetic forces, which try to expand and deform the coil. Testing at 34 kA first let engineers measure the coil's deformation under load and compare it with structural models, check joint resistance and heating and the quench-detection system, and gain operating experience before the much harder full-current test. As lead mechanical engineer Adrien Deydier put it: "It is an important step to reach 34 kA and to confirm the coil is behaving as predicted by the models."

## A magnet the size of a building

Each toroidal field coil is approximately 330 tonnes and measures 9 by 17 metres. It is wound from niobium-tin (Nb₃Sn) superconducting cable — strands bundled inside a steel jacket, wound in seven "double pancake" layers and encased in a stainless-steel structure. Cooled with supercritical helium to around 4 K, the Nb₃Sn becomes superconducting and can carry 68,000 amps with essentially no electrical resistance, generating magnetic fields no copper magnet could sustain. At full current, the coils produce a peak field of 11.8 tesla on the coils and 5.3 tesla at the tokamak's major radius. The full magnet system weighs about 10,000 tonnes and stores 51 gigajoules of magnetic energy, 41 GJ of it in the toroidal field coils alone.

Eighteen of these coils will ring the doughnut-shaped vacuum vessel; together their fields merge into a continuous magnetic field running around the ring. Charged particles in the hot plasma spiral along those field lines instead of flying outward, so the plasma is held in the centre of the vessel without touching the walls. There is also one spare coil, #19, making nineteen in total.

## Why a D?

The D-shape is a structural choice. A current-carrying loop in its own toroidal magnetic field experiences forces that try to expand it, and the "constant-tension" D-shape is the shape a flexible current-carrying conductor would naturally assume — so bending stresses in the conductor are minimised and the load is carried mainly as tension. In ITER, the straight inner leg of each D butts against its neighbours to form a central structural vault, a cylinder at the machine's centre that resists the huge inward force, while the curved outer leg carries the hoop load. Alignment is critical: the coils must be positioned to millimetre precision.

The toroidal field is the main confining field. Six ring-shaped poloidal field coils and the central solenoid add the shaping, position control and plasma current drive.

## Where the programme stands

Testing of coil #17 began in August 2026 at the Magnet Cold Test Facility in Cadarache, southern France — the world's largest superconducting-magnet test bench. It was the facility's second campaign: the first, in May 2026, tested coil #7 at only 10 kA, so the #17 test at 34 kA was a large step up in load. After a brief period in temporary storage, coil #17 will go to the Assembly Hall and be integrated into the plasma chamber's final sector module. It has not yet been installed in the tokamak.

Next in line is spare coil #19, due for cooldown to 4 K from April 2027 and testing at 34 kA, with connections replicating the tokamak's to test high-voltage ground insulation. Only after a reinforcement structure is installed in the test facility will a coil be tested at the full 68 kA. After that, the facility will be reconfigured to test poloidal field coil #1.

One wording note matters for the whole story: the 150-million-degree plasma these magnets will confine is a design target, not something ITER has produced. First plasma and scientific operations are planned for the 2030s under the 2024 baseline. The magnet test is real progress toward it — but the plasma it will one day hold does not exist yet.
