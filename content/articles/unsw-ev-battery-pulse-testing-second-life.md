Retired electric-vehicle batteries are a growing problem and a growing resource. Around 250 million EVs are expected on the roads by 2030, and up to 95% of a battery's components can be recovered — yet only a small fraction of Australia's lithium-ion batteries are collected for recycling. A big reason is testing: deciding whether a used pack is fit for a second life in energy storage, or should go straight to recycling, currently takes a full charge–discharge cycle of about 10 hours and costs roughly $120 per pack, with an overheating and fire risk for damaged cells.

A team at UNSW Sydney, working with battery recycling and lifecycle-management company DY New Energy, is developing a much faster way. Their prototype sends short electrical pulses through each battery cell and measures the response, with statistical models estimating the cell's health from that signal. In lab testing the check takes under three minutes per cell, with accuracy above 90%.

## Per cell, not per pack

Two numbers in the headlines need care. The "under three minutes" figure is per cell, not per pack — a pack contains many cells, so a full-pack check will take longer than three minutes. And the sub-$25 cost is a target, not a demonstrated result: the team aims to bring costs below $25 per pack. The project is an AU$1.3 million effort supported by the Australian Government's Trailblazer for Recycling and Clean Energy (TRaCE) Lab to Market Fund, led by Dr Ziyang Lyu (School of Mathematics and Statistics) and Associate Professor Huadong Mo (School of Systems and Computing). It builds on Mo's earlier AEA Ignite work, which had already cut testing time to five minutes.

The method is designed to work across the three main lithium-ion chemistries — LFP, NCA and NMC — including mixed batches, which matters because recycling streams rarely arrive neatly sorted.

## The factory floor is the next test, not the finished one

The lab results are promising, but the team is clear that industry adoption depends on real-world proof. As Lyu puts it: "Lab results are promising, but industry needs to see the technology perform on the factory floor… before adopting it." The next step is validating the method across more than 500 batteries in real operating conditions, before piloting it in recycling facilities and second-life energy-storage systems.

Also still to come, rather than built: an AI-powered decision engine to recommend reuse versus recycling, and an intelligent Battery Management System (iBMS) module. If the 500-battery trial holds up, the pulse test could make second-life screening fast and cheap enough to do at scale — turning a bottleneck in the battery lifecycle into a routine check.
