Nuclear chemistry is usually studied after the fact: run the reaction, then analyse what came out. Scientists at Argonne National Laboratory have instead watched a plutonium reaction as it happened, using the intense X-rays of the Advanced Photon Source (APS) — described by Argonne as the world's brightest synchrotron X-ray light source and a DOE Office of Science user facility — to directly observe particles form, grow and reorganise during the reaction.

"We successfully performed plutonium precipitation at the APS using our 'lab-in-a-box' containment system," said Matthew Swindall, a postdoctoral appointee in nuclear forensics software development. "We hope that this new capability will open up a whole range of research in nuclear forensics and fuel cycle technologies."

## Why a box was needed at all

The APS enforces strict rules for hazardous substances — multilayer, leak-proof containers and quantity limits — so the team engineered two custom containment boxes:

- **The flow cell** — fully implemented and approved, designed for liquid samples and precipitation reactions. This is the cell type behind the plutonium precipitation work.
- **The heated cell** — still in the design phase, intended to expose radioactive materials to different gas environments at different temperatures.

Two precision points on the headlines: a significant portion of the containment components were 3D printed using readily available materials — 3D-printed components, not necessarily whole printed cells — and of the two cells, only the flow cell is in service so far.

## Validated on a stand-in first

Both systems were first tested with nonradioactive materials at APS beamlines; the release's photo shows a cerium oxalate precipitation experiment at beamline 12-ID-B — cerium being the standard non-radioactive plutonium surrogate. Results aligned with existing data. "It worked the first time," said Anna Servis, Argonne's principal radiochemist. The team — Swindall, Servis, and Derek McLain, group leader for Science & Technology Integration — is now completing the next phase of experiments with radioactive elements. Funding came from Argonne's Laboratory Directed Research and Development (LDRD) program.

One more caveat for the record: Argonne's announcement (17 November 2026) cites no peer-reviewed paper or DOI — the story rests on the lab's news release alone — and the release does not name a specific X-ray technique. What it does establish is a working capability: hazardous nuclear-material reactions, run safely inside a beamline hutch, and watched as they happen.
