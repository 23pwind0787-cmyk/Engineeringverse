Ukraine says it has finished its part of FREYJA, a pan-European anti-ballistic missile defence project named after the Norse goddess. The Ukrainian share — Fire Point's FP-7.X interceptor and its launcher — is complete, President Zelenskyy said on 17 October 2026. But the headline needs its qualifier: the full system is not integrated, not tested, and not ready for deployment. What is done is Ukraine's contribution; the European partners still have components to deliver.

FREYJA is a 10-nation coalition — Ukraine plus Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden and the UK — formally launched in Paris on 13 July 2026, with an 11th country reported to have since joined and a FREYJA office opening in Denmark. It was unveiled in May 2026 with a clear purpose: intercept Russian ballistic missiles, principally the Iskander-M, and end Kyiv's — and Europe's — dependence on US-made Patriot interceptors.

## A system, not just a missile

FREYJA is a full system, not a single weapon. Ukraine's FP-7.X interceptor and launcher must integrate with European radar (Hensoldt's TRML-4D), an infrared seeker (Diehl Defence), a fire-control radar (Weibel/Leonardo) and a command centre (Kongsberg, maker of NASAMS), tied into Ukraine's wider air-defence network over NATO's Link 16. The seeker remains the bottleneck. Denmark has reportedly completed the radar component; the rest is still to come.

The interceptor itself is a faster variant derived from Fire Point's FP-7 strike missile, which saw its first combat use on 1 October 2026. Fire Point, a Ukrainian defence company founded in Kyiv in 2022, is prime contractor and design authority for the FP-7.X and its launcher. The missile is 7.25 m long, with an engagement radius of about 80 km (roughly 50 miles) and an engagement altitude of 20–25 km. It carries a blast-fragmentation warhead initially, with a hit-to-kill version planned later, and a combined infrared-plus-radar seeker.

Two figures need care. The Mach 6.5 top speed is the mature-programme target, not today's spec: Fire Point's current published spec card lists 1,500–2,000 m/s (Mach 4.4–5.9), with speed expected to rise to about 2,200 m/s (Mach 6.5) as the programme matures. And the price — about $1 million per missile — is a serial-production target, not today's cost: Fire Point targets under €1 million (about $1.14 million) per interceptor in serial production, with other statements citing roughly $700,000 per intercept. For comparison, a Patriot PAC-3 interceptor costs about $3.8 million. The production aim is about 2,000 interceptors a year once the line is spun up.

## What happens next

Fire Point hopes to conduct the system's first ballistic missile interception test by around July 2027, with initial system tests targeted for the end of 2026 and a first prototype wanted operational by mid-2027. All of these are targets, not scheduled dates — no precise test date has been announced. As hromadske put it: "The system is not yet operational, and its ability to intercept ballistic missiles in combat has not been demonstrated publicly."

That is the honest state of FREYJA: a European coalition with a finished Ukrainian interceptor at its centre, a credible and much cheaper alternative to Patriot on paper, and everything still to prove in a live interception test.
