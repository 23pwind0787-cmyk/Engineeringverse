## The problem: MOFs are useful, but too fragile to shape

Metal–organic frameworks, or MOFs, are crystalline materials built from metal centres joined by organic linkers. Their ultrahigh porosity and tuneable structures give them an enormous internal surface area, which makes them attractive for gas storage, chemical sensing and electronic devices. But that same open, delicate structure is a weakness when you try to shape them: conventional dry etching with an ion beam shatters the crystal structure, so the standard workaround has been cryo-FIB — cooling the material with liquid nitrogen during etching. That adds cost, complexity and specialised equipment.

## The fix: fill the pores with a liquid salt

A team led by Assistant Professor Yumeng Zheng and Professor Kentaro Kinoshita from the Department of Applied Physics at Tokyo University of Science tried a simpler idea: reinforce the framework from the inside before etching it. They infiltrated Cu3BTC2 — a well-studied copper-based MOF also known as HKUST-1, in which copper centres are joined by benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylic acid linkers — with four imidazolium-based ionic liquids. Ionic liquids are salts that stay liquid near room temperature, and they filled 50–60% of the framework's pore volume without changing its crystallinity.

The infiltrated material was measurably harder and more stable, and it survived a focused gallium ion beam at room temperature. The study, published in *Communications Materials*, reports that the ionic-liquid-assisted process etched fine patterns at the hundred-nanometre scale with no liquid nitrogen and no damage to the microstructure.

## What they carved

Using the method, the team patterned a 5 × 5 micrometre square, freestanding pillars and even a Chinese character with etched lines 130–300 nanometres wide. They also demonstrated fine patterning with sub-100-nanometre precision at room temperature — a resolution that previously required cooling the sample.

## Why it matters

Patterning is the step that turns a promising material into a working device. If MOFs can be shaped precisely at room temperature with ordinary etching equipment, it becomes far more practical to build MOF-based sensors and electronic components. The work was supported in part by JSPS KAKENHI Grant 26K00987.

One honest limit: the demonstration so far is on a single MOF, Cu3BTC2, in the laboratory. Turning the technique into a manufacturing process for real devices is still future work — but removing the need for cryogenic cooling takes away one of the biggest practical barriers.
