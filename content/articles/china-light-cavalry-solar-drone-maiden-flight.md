China's first commercial-grade solar-powered drone has completed its maiden flight. The aircraft, called Light Cavalry, took off, climbed, circled and landed safely in Northwest China on 11 November 2026, passing key tests of its flight controls, avionics, energy storage system, stability and handling.

Developed by the China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation's 11th Research Institute, the fully solar-powered unmanned aerial vehicle is designed for commercial missions that need sustained low-altitude coverage — and its first flight marks the transition of solar-powered UAV technology from the laboratory to the market.

## A small airframe with a long reach

Light Cavalry measures 3.2 metres long with an 8-metre wingspan. It has a takeoff weight of 45 kilograms, a service ceiling of 7,000 metres and a maximum design endurance of 30 hours. It can carry more than 5 kilograms of equipment — over 11 pounds — and the payload can be swapped to suit different assignments: electro-optical instruments for observation, communications equipment or detection systems.

That flexibility lets operators use the same airframe for jobs ranging from land surveys to ecological monitoring. The developer sees potential uses in infrastructure inspection, water conservancy monitoring, disaster assessment and large-scale surveillance of coastlines and ports.

## No runway needed

The drone uses a vehicle-mounted, runway-independent takeoff system and takes about 20 minutes to prepare on the ground. Launching without a fixed runway allows crews to work where airfields are scarce — mountainous regions, remote coastlines and wilderness areas — and to choose a departure point closer to the area they need to survey.

## Why a small solar drone is hard

Most development of solar-powered drones has so far focused on large and ultra-large aircraft, which serve mainly as research platforms or high-altitude pseudo-satellites. Smaller aircraft could serve a wider range of commercial jobs closer to the ground, but their size makes powering them entirely with sunlight harder: a smaller airframe offers less surface area for solar panels, while the structure, avionics and energy storage equipment still account for a substantial share of its weight.

Improvements in materials, motors and energy storage could help close the gap between what the panels generate and what the aircraft needs to stay aloft. The institute expects Light Cavalry to fill a gap in the market for long-endurance, low-altitude aircraft — extending flight times and reducing operational carbon emissions on missions that require sustained coverage over large areas.
