SpaceX has caught a Starship upper stage returning from orbit for the first time. Ship 42 settled into the Starbase tower's Mechazilla "chopstick" arms on Saturday 14 November 2026, ending a 22.5-hour, 14-orbit flight. SpaceX confirmed it on X: "Mechazilla catches Starship!"

Flight 15, the 15th integrated test flight, lifted off from Pad 2 at Starbase on Friday 13 November at 22:47 UTC. Ship 42 — the 52-metre upper stage — stayed in orbit for the full 14 orbits at about 260 km altitude, deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites about 35–40 minutes after launch, and then came home. Three of the deployed satellites carried cameras to photograph Ship 42's heat shield, and SpaceX said the images showed it in good condition. The ship cruised at about 17,000 mph before a roughly 10-second deorbit burn, re-entered over the Pacific, flew supersonically across Mexico and the southwestern US, then turned out over the ocean to line up with the tower, reignited three Raptor engines for the final descent, flipped vertical and settled into the arms at about 21:20 UTC — about 22 hours 33 minutes after liftoff.

## One catch out of two

This was the first attempt to catch both stages at the tower, and only the Ship made it — so it is not a complete double-catch success. Super Heavy boosters had already been caught three times before this flight; the first upper-stage catch is the new milestone, and only the second time a Starship has reached orbit at all.

The booster, B22 — the first Version 3 Super Heavy — was due back at Pad 2 about 6.5 minutes after liftoff. Its boostback burn ended early on a low-fuel-mass alarm, and its guidance system then triggered an automated abort during coast and diverted it offshore. SpaceX's own wording was "diverted to an offshore splashdown in the Gulf", but Spaceflight Now, CNBC and PBS all report the booster exploded before hitting the water, its flight-termination system firing, with debris left in the Gulf ringed by marker buoys. The root cause is still under investigation. A Version 3 booster has still not made a successful return.

The flight still moves SpaceX's reusability programme forward: catching the upper stage was the harder half of the plan, and it worked on the first attempt. But the booster failure shows the Version 3 vehicle still has a return problem to solve before the double catch — and the rapid, fully reusable launch cadence it promises — is real.
