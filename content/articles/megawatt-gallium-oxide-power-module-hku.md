## What was built

A team from the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering and the Centre for Advanced Semiconductors and Integrated Circuits (CASIC) at the University of Hong Kong has demonstrated a gallium oxide (Ga2O3) power module capable of continuous pulsed switching at 1,000 volts and 1,000 amperes — a peak power level of one megawatt. It is the first time an ultrawide-bandgap (UWBG) multi-chip module has reached the megawatt level.

The work was led by Professor Yuhao Zhang, with Dr. Hehe Gong and Ph.D. student Xin Yang, in collaboration with Professor Guo-Quan Lu of Virginia Tech and Professor Jiandong Ye of Nanjing University. It is published in *Nature Communications*.

## The numbers

- A single packaged Ga2O3 device conducted 234 amperes at 1,000 volts in pulsed operation, while surviving junction temperatures above 250 °C.
- Six of those devices integrated into one module switched 1,000 V and 1,000 A repeatedly at a frequency of 1 kHz.
- Switching took about 23 nanoseconds, with near-zero reverse recovery.
- The module reached a pulsed power capacity density above 1.8 MW/cm².

The team describes the result as more than a two-order-of-magnitude increase in the power capability previously demonstrated with UWBG semiconductors — a field whose practical power levels had stayed in the kilowatt range for a decade, held back by material and device non-uniformity and by packaging challenges.

## How they did it

The advance is a device-package co-design rather than a new material alone. Gallium oxide has a bandgap of about 4.8 eV, which in principle allows very high voltages with low losses, but the material conducts heat poorly. The team built a junction-side cooling architecture with a high-permittivity interface: using the polarisation effect of high-permittivity layers, they redistributed the electric field, raising breakdown voltages while cutting thermal resistance.

The target application is pulsed power — systems that must handle extreme voltage and current within microsecond timescales, such as medical equipment, power grids and fusion energy. Gallium oxide's high volumetric heat capacity and thermal stability suit that regime, because transient heating in pulsed systems is dominated by volumetric heat capacity.

## Recognition and the road ahead

An earlier version of the work was selected as a Technical Highlight at the 2025 IEEE International Electron Devices Meeting (IEDM), a distinction reserved for the top 1.5% of submissions, and *Nature Electronics* published a News & Views commentary on the team's approach by Professor Savannah Eisner of Columbia University.

The honest caveat: this is a research demonstration of pulsed switching, not a commercial product. Gallium oxide power devices are still in development, and the module's megawatt figure is a peak pulsed rating rather than continuous power delivery. But as a proof that UWBG modules can scale past the kilowatt ceiling that held for ten years, it is a significant step for high-power electronics in grids, electric vehicles, data centres and fusion systems.
