About 136 km off China's southern coast, in water 136 metres deep, a wind turbine taller than a 110-storey building is now generating power — while floating. CNOOC has switched on Haiyou Anlan, the world's first 16 MW wind turbine on a tension-leg floating platform, and all of its electricity goes to an offshore oilfield.

The platform entered official operation on 6 August 2026, when it was connected to the Lufeng oilfield power grid in the Pearl River Mouth Basin, about 200 km southeast of Shenzhen. It is China's first tension-leg floating wind platform and the world's largest of its kind by turbine capacity, operating at the greatest water depth and farthest distance from shore of any tension-leg platform yet built.

## Held down, not held up

A tension-leg platform works the opposite way from most floating structures. Its hull is built to float higher than it sits in the water, and nine steel tendons anchored to the seabed pull it down and hold it taut. The tension restrains the platform's movement in every direction, keeping it almost perfectly still — which is what allows such a large turbine to operate safely on a floating base.

The numbers show the scale. Haiyou Anlan stands over 307 metres tall and weighs 7,800 tonnes, with a maximum displacement exceeding 15,000 tonnes. Its 16 MW typhoon-resistant turbine, built by Mingyang, has a rotor diameter of 260 metres and blades that sweep an area equivalent to 7.5 standard football fields. The platform is designed to survive Category-17 typhoons, with sustained winds up to about 220 km/h, and has a service life of 25 years.

Compared with the semi-submersible floating platforms widely used elsewhere, the tension-leg design cuts steel consumption by 40% and seabed occupation area by 90%, according to the builder, Offshore Oil Engineering Co. (COOEC). Its design concept received Approval in Principle certifications from four leading classification societies: CCS, BV, ABS and DNV.

## Powering an oilfield — and a question about data centres

Once fully operational, Haiyou Anlan is expected to supply 54 million kilowatt-hours of green electricity to the oilfield each year, saving about 15,000 cubic metres of fuel oil and reducing carbon dioxide emissions by 35,000 tonnes. The platform's hull was built at COOEC's Qingdao fabrication yard, towed to the company's Zhuhai yard for final assembly, and sailed from Gaolan Port on 28 June 2026.

The project also raises a wider question, which Interesting Engineering's video on the platform explores: if floating wind can work in deep, typhoon-prone water, could it eventually power offshore data centres too? That is a future possibility, not something Haiyou Anlan does today — its power goes to the oilfield. But the platform shows that the technology for deep-water floating wind at this scale now exists, and that is what makes the data-centre question worth asking.
