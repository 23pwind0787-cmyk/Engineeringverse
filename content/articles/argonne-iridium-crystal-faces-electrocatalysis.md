The same metal can tell a different chemical story depending on which face it shows. Scientists at Argonne National Laboratory compared three crystal faces of iridium in acidic and alkaline solutions, and found that each face drives key energy reactions differently — producing benchmark data that could help design better catalysts.

Within a single metal crystal, faces are flat surfaces that expose atoms in different geometric patterns. Cut the crystal one way and the atoms are packed tightly; cut it another and they sit further apart or in rows. The atoms are the same element, but the chemistry changes with how they are arranged.

> "Think of a cut diamond. Each face can reflect light differently. Here, each crystal face gives you a different chemistry." — Pietro Papa Lopes, electrochemist at Argonne National Laboratory

## Four reactions, two environments

Iridium is a silvery-white transition metal in the same group as platinum and gold, and it is widely used as an electrocatalyst — a material that speeds up reactions at electrode surfaces. But its behaviour in metallic form is far less understood than platinum's, which makes it harder to predict how it will perform.

To build a benchmark, the Argonne team exposed three well-defined iridium crystal faces to acidic and alkaline solutions and tested them in four reactions that matter to electrochemical technologies:

- **Making hydrogen** — the hydrogen evolution reaction, central to green hydrogen production.
- **Using hydrogen as fuel** — the hydrogen oxidation reaction, the anode reaction in fuel cells.
- **Oxygen reduction** — adding electrons to oxygen, a key reaction in fuel cells.
- **Handling hydrogen peroxide** — an unwanted side product that can lower efficiency and damage devices.

Comparing acidic and alkaline conditions matters because both are widely used in electrochemical technologies, and each changes how reactions happen at a metal surface. Acidic liquids generally conduct electricity more efficiently, but many materials are not stable in acid. Alkaline liquids can be less corrosive and may allow cheaper materials, though they come with trade-offs of their own.

## No single face wins

The central finding is that no single iridium surface performed best across all reactions — and the surrounding liquid could strongly change the metal's behaviour. One face was better for the hydrogen-related reactions, while a different face was better for oxygen reduction.

The hydrogen reactions became much slower in alkaline conditions: there, the surface first has to split water molecules to get hydrogen, which takes more energy. In acid, the surface can draw hydrogen directly from the plentiful protons in the liquid.

Oxygen reduction behaved differently. Its overall reaction speed was less affected by whether the solution was acidic or alkaline, but the products it formed changed sharply. In acidic conditions, the iridium surfaces produced almost no hydrogen peroxide. In alkaline conditions, hydrogen peroxide production increased sharply under some conditions — a problem, because excess hydrogen peroxide lowers energy efficiency and can damage parts of electrochemical devices.

> "An important contribution of this research is not just whether iridium is a good or bad material overall. It's that we are providing the community with benchmark reactivity data for well-defined iridium surfaces." — Pietro Papa Lopes

The results show that catalytic performance depends not only on the metal itself but also on its environment. By using carefully controlled crystal surfaces, the researchers could isolate the behaviour of specific iridium structures — and with that data, scientists can compare iridium with more widely used catalyst materials such as platinum and design improved catalysts for energy technologies.

The study was funded through the US Department of Energy's Early Career Research Program, and its results were published in the journal *ACS Catalysis*. Other contributors include Kavyasree Anjanarambath, who took part as a doctoral student at the University of Illinois Chicago, and Kaline Nascimento da Silva, an Argonne postdoctoral appointee.
