A German university has turned its car park into a power station. The Diepholz campus of the Private University of Applied Sciences for Business and Technology (PHWT) inaugurated a new solar carport on 9 November 2026, covering 90 parking spaces and paired with the first sodium-ion battery storage system in the state of Lower Saxony.

The installation combines about 237 kWp of photovoltaic capacity with a 200 kWh sodium-ion battery, letting the campus store its own solar power for later use and rely less on the grid.

## A semicircular roof that is the solar panel

The car park's most striking feature is its shape: the existing lot is arranged in a semicircle with two rows of spaces, and the university wanted the roof to follow that unusual geometry. Solar company PfefferKnecht designed a custom metal structure for the curve — the first of its kind — and local engineering firm Böckmann Maschinenbau, a longstanding university partner, erected it.

The carport roof covers roughly 1,150 square metres with glass-glass photovoltaic modules, providing around 230 kWp. A bicycle shelter for 20 bikes adds another 7.3 kWp, bringing the project's total to about 237 kWp. The modules themselves are the roof: a patented feature is that the individual photovoltaic modules also serve as a seamless, waterproof roof covering, so no additional roofing membrane is needed underneath. Integrated LED light strips illuminate the covered spaces.

## Salt instead of lithium

The storage system is the project's other first: two sodium-ion battery cabinets supplied by Swiss green energy company Phenogy, with 200 kWh of storage capacity and 100 kW of charging and discharging power. It is the first sodium-ion storage system in Lower Saxony and the third in Germany.

Sodium-ion technology uses widely available sodium-based materials and needs no lithium, graphite or cobalt. That makes the batteries barely flammable and resistant to high and low temperatures without losing performance — a practical advantage for an outdoor installation in northern Germany.

Stored electricity can cover peak demand periods instead of buying expensive power from the grid, and the university expects the system to reduce its energy costs as well as its grid dependence.

## Ten weeks to build

The project took nearly 18 months from first development to completion, but the actual construction was squeezed into a 10-week window during the semester break — foundations, steel frame, solar panels and electrical systems — so the parking spaces were available again in time for the new semester. The effort was initiated by PHWT president Professor Dennis De and PfefferKnecht managing director Andreas Knecht; PfefferKnecht planned and built the solar structures under its Lichtkraft brand.

The carport complements an existing sustainable energy concept at the Diepholz campus, which already uses an ice-based thermal storage system for heating and cooling. Combined with photovoltaics and the new battery, the university says it is now a leader in sustainable energy well beyond Lower Saxony.
