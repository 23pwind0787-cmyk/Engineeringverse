The James Webb Space Telescope has caught a supernova that exploded 8.8 billion years ago — and thanks to a cosmic magnifying glass, astronomers will get to watch it explode twice more, in about 73 and 76 years.

The supernova, named SN Ares, was found in JWST near-infrared (NIRCam) images of the galaxy-cluster field MACS J0308.9+2645 taken in August 2025, with NIRSpec spectroscopy following on 23 September 2025. Its spectrum shows helium features alongside hydrogen lines — the signature of a Type IIb supernova, a massive star that lost most, but not all, of its outer hydrogen envelope before exploding, likely stripped by a companion star. At redshift 1.276, it is the highest-redshift Type IIb supernova ever identified from a spectrum. The light left when the universe was 4.9 billion years old.

## A lens with a 70-year delay

MACS J0308.9+2645 is a massive cluster of hundreds of galaxies. Its gravity bends the supernova's light into three paths of different lengths through different gravitational fields, so the same explosion reaches Earth as three images at different times. SN Ares's host galaxy sits almost directly behind the cluster and is split into three images.

The team's best lens models predict the second image will arrive in about 73 years and the third in about 76 years after the one already seen — by far the longest predicted delays of any lensed supernova system discovered so far. Those numbers are model predictions, not exact dates: the models span roughly 70–76 years, they assume a Hubble constant of 70 km/s/Mpc, and a change of 3 km/s/Mpc in that constant shifts the delays by about 5 years.

That is exactly why the system matters. The delay between image arrivals depends on the geometry of space and the expansion rate, so measuring the gap between the images constrains the Hubble constant, H₀ — the local expansion rate of the universe. Because a supernova light curve has a sharp, recognisable feature (its radioactive peak), the arrival times can be timed precisely. Earlier lensed supernovae — Refsdal, H0pe, Encore — gave H₀ values spanning 65–76 km/s/Mpc, the range at the heart of cosmology's current tension over the expansion rate. Ares's ~70-year baseline could, if the return is caught in the 2090s, give one of the most precise single H₀ measurements ever.

The work is led by Conor Larison, a postdoctoral research fellow at the Space Telescope Science Institute, with more than 70 co-authors. One note on status: the paper was posted to arXiv on 23 October 2026 and submitted to *The Astrophysical Journal* — it is a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed. Planned JWST/NIRSpec spectroscopy of the cluster in early 2027 should tighten the lens model and the delay predictions.
