Green hydrogen is made by splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen using electricity from renewable sources — but tiny bubbles formed during the process waste energy. Now, researchers at Cornell University and City University of Hong Kong have built a three-dimensional computer model that reveals exactly how bubbles interfere, and identifies a critical bubble size beyond which the energy losses rise steeply.

Their findings, published on 9 November 2026 in the journal *EES Catalysis*, could provide a roadmap for designing more efficient hydrogen-producing equipment.

## Why bubbles waste energy

As electricity passes through an electrolyzer, hydrogen and oxygen bubbles develop on the electrodes where the chemical reactions take place. The bubbles can block the active reaction sites, keep water from reaching the electrode surfaces, or interfere with the movement of charged particles — so the electrolyzer uses more electricity to produce the same hydrogen.

Research teams have long experimented with changing electrode shapes, adjusting liquid flows and modifying surface properties to help bubbles detach easily. But calculating the precise energy lost to individual bubbles has remained difficult.

> "Experimentally it's really challenging to isolate the impact of bubbles on each individual scale." — Lenan Zhang, assistant professor at Cornell University's Sibley School of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering

## A digital twin across eight orders of magnitude

To address that, the team built a three-dimensional mathematical modelling framework capable of simulating the interconnected processes across eight orders of magnitude in length — from the nanometre scale of the electrochemical double layer to a centimetre-scale cell, with micro- to millimetre-scale bubbles in between. It works like a digital twin of an electrolyzer: it can reproduce real electrode shapes, materials and operating conditions while revealing microscopic processes that are difficult to observe experimentally.

The approach also cuts the computational effort needed to run the simulations by nearly 1,000 times, without compromising numerical accuracy.

Using the model to examine individual hydrogen bubbles forming on an electrode in an alkaline electrolyzer, the researchers found that the largest source of bubble-induced energy loss is the bubbles blocking active electrode surface area. More importantly, they identified a critical bubble radius. Once a bubble exceeds that size, the additional voltage required to sustain electrolysis increases exponentially. In certain modelled conditions, the total additional voltage can exceed its bubble-free value by more than 50%.

The critical radius depends on electrode geometry and how readily liquid wets the electrode surface — rather than on the operating current density, which is a useful simplification for designers.

## Designing bubbles away

The team also introduced a new design parameter, comparing the radius at which bubbles detach from the electrode with the critical radius. Their simulations indicate that keeping this ratio below one — detaching bubbles before they reach the critical size — could limit the additional voltage required due to an individual bubble to within 3%.

That points to a practical design rule: build electrodes that release bubbles before they grow too large. The team now plans to expand the model to test how multiple bubbles grow, merge and interact, and to combine it with computational optimisation and 3D printing to develop more efficient electrode designs.

The study was led by Lenan Zhang, assistant professor in Cornell's Sibley School of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, and Weihong Li, associate professor of mechanical engineering at City University of Hong Kong. Muhan Zhang of City University of Hong Kong and Kaiyu Li, a visiting undergraduate student at Cornell, contributed equally and are co-first authors. The paper is "Double-layer-resolved full-field multiscale simulation unraveling impacts of bubbles on water electrolysis", published in *EES Catalysis* (DOI: 10.1039/D6EY00288A).
